# Sign the capture, let the pixels point

AI-generated fake video and imagery is already a serious problem in 2025–2026, and every available indicator says it will grow. Documented deepfake incidents roughly quadrupled from 2024 to the first half of 2025. Frontier video generators now make clips that people judge correctly at around chance. Mainstream platforms produced mass abuse imagery in early 2026. And politicians now use the "liar's dividend": they wave away real footage as "probably AI." Media-provenance technology is better than it has ever been, but it does not yet protect footage once it is shared. C2PA added live-video signing and watermark-based manifest recovery. Google's Pixel 10 signs every photo with hardware-attested keys, and Sony signs newsroom video. Yet in October 2025 none of eight major platforms kept Content Credentials on an uploaded video. Signing keys and validators have also failed in practice: Nikon had to revoke every certificate it had issued. PixelRoot moves trust into the pixels and onto a ledger, which targets the right gap: provenance that survives stripping and transcoding. As submitted, though, it does not meet its own five goals. Its in-pixel mark can be transplanted onto other content, and its own experiment shows this. Its security proposition does not match its code. It has no video experiments. It is also built from too many parts to be "extremely simple." For IEEE TIFS my recommendation is **major revision, leaning reject-and-resubmit**. The way forward is to make PixelRoot smaller. The hardware-signed C2PA manifest, hashed per video segment, should be the primary binding. The in-pixel mark should carry only a short random lookup ID that points to that manifest. A perceptual fingerprint should serve as the backup lookup, and a public multi-operator transparency log should replace the mandatory blockchain. Viewers should get a neutral "no information" state and a link back to the original footage.

## Deepfake misinformation is pressing now and the curve is still steepening

All the counts below come from trackers that log documented cases, so they are floors, and most come from vendors who sell detection. Even so, they all point the same way. Resemble AI counted **1,567 verified deepfake incidents and about $1.28B in documented fraud losses in 2025**. It notes that more than 80% of incidents disclosed no financial figure, so the true losses are higher ([Resemble AI 2025 report](https://resemble.ai/2025-deepfake-threat-report)). Surfshark counted **about 580 incidents in H1 2025, nearly four times the roughly 150 in all of 2024** ([Surfshark](https://surfshark.com/research/chart/deepfake-fraud-losses)). Fraud is getting better rather than just more frequent. Sumsub found that multi-step, AI-driven fraud rose from 10% to 28% of identity fraud between 2024 and 2025, while the overall fraud rate fell slightly ([Fintech News SG](https://fintechnews.sg/sumsub-identity-fraud-report-2025-2026/)). The benchmark corporate case remains Arup. In 2024 it lost about US$25.6M after an employee joined a video call in which the "CFO" and other colleagues were all deepfakes ([CNN](https://edition.cnn.com/2024/05/16/tech/arup-deepfake-scam-loss-hong-kong-intl-hnk)). The largest harm by volume came in early 2026, from a mainstream tool rather than a fringe one. The Center for Countering Digital Hate estimated that xAI's Grok produced **about 3M sexualized images on X in 11 days, about 23,000 of them depicting children**. Probes or bans followed in the UK, EU, France, Australia, Indonesia, Malaysia and the Philippines ([Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/355543/grok-generated-23k-sexualized-images-children-watchdog-reports)).

Generation capability explains why these numbers keep rising. NewsGuard prompted OpenAI's Sora 2 with 20 provably false claims. It produced realistic videos for **16 of them (80%)**, each in minutes. Sora's visible watermark could be removed in under four minutes with free tools ([NewsGuard](https://www.newsguardtech.com/special-reports/openai-sora-seeing-should-not-be-believing/)). Human judgment no longer works as a filter. In one study of Veo 3, LTX and Wan clips, people identified text-to-video fakes correctly only **43%** of the time, below chance ([UNIST](https://scholarworks.unist.ac.kr/handle/201301/90247)). In a December 2025 benchmark, viewers judged Veo 3.1-Fast output fake only **12.54%** of the time ([arXiv 2512.13281](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.13281)). Automated detectors do not close the gap. On real-world 2024 deepfakes, open-source detectors lost **about 50% of their AUC for video** compared with academic benchmarks ([Deepfake-Eval-2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.02857v1)). Open-weight models such as Wan 2.2 (Apache 2.0) put comparable generation on consumer hardware with no safeguards ([Thunder Compute](https://www.thundercompute.com/blog/best-open-source-ai-video-generation-models)).

The second harm is the one provenance is best placed to fix: real footage being dismissed as fake. In September 2025 the US President said a video of an object thrown from a White House window was "probably AI," even though his staff had told reporters it was real ([Euronews](https://euronews.com/next/2025/09/04/donald-trump-blames-ai-for-video-showing-items-thrown-from-white-house-is-it-the-new-fake-)). Courts face the mirror image. A California judge dismissed *Mendones v. Cushman & Wakefield* after finding deepfake witness video among the exhibits, and noted the court lacked "the time, funding, or technical expertise" for forensic analysis ([NBC News](https://www.nbcwashington.com/news/national-international/ai-generated-evidence-showing-up-in-court-alarms-judges/4017477/)). Real-world misinformation is not yet mostly AI. During the June 2025 Israel–Iran war, AI clips made with Veo 3 went viral within weeks of its release, but recycled old footage still dominated ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/world/mena/tech-fuelled-misinformation-distorts-iran-israel-fighting-1.500171436); [Hespress](https://en.hespress.com/113702-ai-generated-videos-fuel-disinformation-amid-iran-israel-conflict-experts-warn.html)). That matters for design. A provenance system that lets people find the *original, dated* footage counters both recycling and synthesis.

Forecasts point to continued growth. Deloitte projects US gen-AI-enabled fraud rising from **$12.3B in 2023 to $40B by 2027** ([Deloitte](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/financial-services/deepfake-banking-fraud-risk-on-the-rise.html)). The WEF's 2026 Global Risks Report ranks misinformation and disinformation **#2 on the two-year outlook** ([Statista](https://statista.com/chart/35680/major-global-risks-short-and-long-term-in-2026/)). In the Reuters Institute's 2025 survey, 58% of respondents across 48 markets worried about telling true from false online ([GIJN](https://gijn.org/stories/2025-reuters-institute-digital-news-report/)). Some things remain uncertain. No neutral government count of incidents exists. Part of the measured growth reflects more reporting. And there is no representative study of human detection for Sora 2 or Veo 3 with audio. The direction is still clear. Detection alone cannot carry the load, and that is the central argument for capture-time provenance.

## Signing at capture now ships, but distribution still strips it

The C2PA standard moved fast in 2025–2026. Version 2.2 (May 2025) added an API that recovers a stripped manifest from a watermark or fingerprint. Version 2.3 (December 2025) added signing for live DASH/HLS streams ([C2PA spec history](https://spec.c2pa.org/specifications/specifications/2.4/specs/C2PA_Specification.html)). Each live CMAF segment is signed as it is produced, using short-lived session keys. In one proof of concept the bandwidth overhead was about **0.56%** ([Irdeto](https://irdeto.com/blog/extending-trust-into-live-video-with-c2pa); [Unified Streaming](https://www.unified-streaming.com/presentations/when-a-man-in-the-middle-tries-crashing-the-live-streaming-party-the-c2pa-bouncer-steps-in)). A Conformance Program with assurance levels soft-launched in June 2025, and the Interim Trust List was frozen on 1 January 2026 ([Trust over IP](https://www.trustoverip.org/blog/2025/05/20/egwg-2025-05-15-the-c2pa-conformance-program-scott-perry/); [softwareseni](https://www.softwareseni.com/the-c2pa-trust-layer-in-2026-where-it-works-and-where-it-breaks)). C2PA's answer to stripping is "Durable Content Credentials": a signed manifest, plus an invisible watermark, plus a fingerprint, so the manifest can be found again after metadata is removed ([CAI](https://contentauthenticity.org/blog/three-pillars-of-provenance)). The official soft-binding list already includes Digimarc's watermark (images, audio and video), Adobe TrustMark (images) and ISCC fingerprinting (video) ([C2PA soft-binding list](https://spec.c2pa.org/softbinding-alg-list/softbinding-algorithm-list.json)). In other words, the architecture PixelRoot proposes already exists as a standard. What does not exist yet is a *video-robust, attack-resistant* soft binding and the infrastructure to resolve it.

On devices, the **Google Pixel 10 is the first mass-market phone to sign every photo by default** at C2PA Assurance Level 2. It uses Titan M2 hardware attestation, on-device trusted timestamps, and one-time keys so that a user's images cannot be linked to each other ([Google Security Blog](https://security.googleblog.com/2025/09/pixel-android-trusted-images-c2pa-content-credentials.html?hl=fr); [The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2025/09/google-pixel-10-adds-c2pa-support-to.html?hl=en)). There is no confirmed Pixel 10 *video* signing. For video, the only well-documented capture signer is Sony's paid newsroom service on Alpha and FX bodies, launched in October 2025. Its Camera Verify added video verification in March 2026. It accepts MP4 only up to 222 Mbps and adds 3D-depth checks to detect re-filmed screens ([Newsshooter](https://www.newsshooter.com/2026/03/12/sonys-camera-authenticity-solution-now-supports-video/); [Sony](https://www.sony.eu/presscentre/enhancing-content-integrity-sony-launches-comprehensive-video-authenticity-verification-for-news-media)). Samsung signs only images edited with Galaxy AI tools ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/01/22/samsung-unpacked-samsungs-galaxy-s25-will-support-content-credentials-to-identify-ai-generated-images)). Apple ships no C2PA. **Consumer video provenance at the point of capture is essentially absent in October 2026.**

Distribution is where provenance currently breaks. In the Washington Post's October 2025 test, an AI video carrying Content Credentials was uploaded to eight major platforms. **None kept the credentials or let users reach them**, and only YouTube disclosed anything, inside the expanded description ([Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2025/10/22/ai-deepfake-sora-platforms-c2pa/)). Cloudflare Images can preserve credentials through image transformations, but no video equivalent was found ([Cloudflare](https://developers.cloudflare.com/images/optimization/transformations/preserve-content-credentials/)). The trust layer has also failed in the field. Nikon's Z6 III multiple-exposure mode let fabricated images receive valid signatures. Nikon suspended its service and revoked every certificate it had issued, and standard validators did not check revocation anyway ([PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2025/09/22/nikon-cant-fully-solve-the-z6-iiis-c2pa-problems-alone/); [heise](https://heise.de/-10667707)). Formal analyses in 2026 found further gaps. Timestamps can be replaced undetected, and a revoked certificate was still reported as valid ([arXiv 2604.24890](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.24890v1)). Metadata and manifest-store bits can change without invalidating the signature ([IACR ePrint 2026/804](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/804.pdf)). In an "Integrity Clash," a valid human-authorship manifest and an AI watermark can both pass on the same pixels ([arXiv 2603.02378](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.02378v1)).

Critics also raise social problems with C2PA. The NSA's January 2025 guidance warns that wide adoption could make the public treat *uncredentialed* media as suspect without justification ([NSA CSI](https://media.defense.gov/2025/Jan/29/2003634788/-1/-1/0/CSI-CONTENT-CREDENTIALS.PDF)). The World Privacy Forum warns that identity data in a distributed manifest can never be withdrawn ([WPF](https://worldprivacyforum.org/posts/privacy-identity-and-trust-in-c2pa/)). WITNESS's "tick" dilemma makes a related point. If verification becomes expected, people on cheap phones, rooted devices or poor connections will be disbelieved ([WITNESS](https://lab.witness.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2019/12/Ticks_or_it_didn%E2%80%99t_happen_WITNESS.pdf)). Several lessons follow for PixelRoot. A hardware signature attests that "this device signed this," not that the scene was real. Revocation and timestamps must be enforced by verifiers. And "no credential" must mean "unknown."

## Watermarks, fingerprints and ledgers each fail differently

Production watermarks survive everyday image edits but are weak against video codecs. Independent tests of Meta's Video Seal under H.264 measured **94.2% bit accuracy at CRF 23, 62.1% at CRF 35, and 50.5% (chance) at CRF 45** ([COVER, arXiv 2609.26236](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.26236)). Meta's own Pixel Seal paper says H.264 and HEVC are harder than any geometric attack ([arXiv 2512.16874](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.16874)). Adobe TrustMark, the open watermark used for Durable Content Credentials, carries only about 100 bits. By default 61 are data bits protected by BCH error correction ([TrustMark docs](https://opensource.contentauthenticity.org/docs/trustmark/python/config)). That is enough for a lookup ID and not for an identity record. Google's SynthID has been applied to more than 10B items, but its verifier is open only to trusted testers ([arXiv 2510.09263](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09263)). Re-filming a screen at good quality remains an unsolved problem for watermarks ([arXiv 2603.26766](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.26766)). No peer-reviewed study measures survival through actual TikTok, YouTube or WhatsApp pipelines. PixelRoot could fill that gap.

The attack literature is decisive for PixelRoot's design. Diffusion regeneration provably removes low-perturbation pixel watermarks ([Zhao et al., NeurIPS 2024](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/10272bfd0371ef960ec557ed6c866058-Abstract.html)). There is a fundamental trade-off between missed detections and false alarms, and black-box spoofing is possible ([Saberi et al., ICLR 2024](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/744e09d3213ee53de88f04164a9b06ab-Abstract-Conference.html)). For a provenance system, though, removal only yields "no information." The dangerous attack is **forgery**. Kutter et al.'s copy attack (2000) estimates a watermark by denoising and pastes it onto another image, with no key needed. The standard defense is to bind the payload to the image content ([Copy attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copy_attack)). Yang et al. showed that simply averaging many marked images reveals a fixed carrier. The recovered carrier can then be used to remove the mark or forge it, and fixed-pattern schemes are the most exposed ([NeurIPS 2024](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/67b2e2e895380fa6acd537c2894e490e-Abstract.html)). One video clip is thousands of frames, which makes video the ideal input for averaging. A gain-modulated sensor mark also has the same physical form as PRNU, the sensor's natural noise fingerprint. Known PRNU fingerprint-copy attacks therefore apply to it directly, and improved attacks defeat the "triangle test" defense ([arXiv 1707.07795](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1707.07795)).

Perceptual fingerprints fail the other way. Nothing is embedded, so nothing can be removed, but they collide easily. People produced NeuralHash collisions within 48 hours of the model being extracted ([arXiv 2110.07450](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.07450)), and USENIX Security 2026 reports black-box exact collisions ([Leblanc-Albarel & Preneel](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity26/presentation/leblanc-albarel)). The defensible position is to use both, and to use them only as **lookup keys into a signed manifest, never as proof by themselves**.

Ledgers add less than their proponents claim. OpenTimestamps batches many stamps into one Bitcoin transaction for almost nothing, but confirmation takes "a few hours." The proof shows only that a hash existed at a given time, not who made it ([PyPI](https://pypi.python.org/pypi/opentimestamps-client); [loxal](https://www.loxal.net/article/tech/BitCoin-notary.html)). The Reuters/Canon/Starling pilot (Hedera, 2023) and Fox Verify (Polygon, 89,000 items at beta) remained pilots and licensing tools ([Thomson Reuters](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en/press-releases/2023/august/reuters-new-proof-of-concept-employs-authentication-system-to-securely-capture-store-and-verify-photographs); [The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/271288/fox-corporation-polygon-labs-verify)). Certificate-Transparency-style logs give public, append-only auditability without consensus fees. Independent witnesses co-sign the log, whereas blockchains are "expensive because of transaction costs and [have] slow finality" ([SoK, arXiv 2305.01378](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.01378)). The trust in every one of these deployments comes from the capture signature, not from the ledger. A periodic checkpoint anchored to a public chain is a cheap optional extra, not a foundation.

The evidence on how users respond to labels supports keeping the interface modest. A 6,114-person study found that C2PA provenance information raised trust in the news source ([ICWSM](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/view/42749)). Another study found that users confuse "provenance is credible" with "content is true" ([arXiv 2303.12118](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.12118)). Meta's 2024 "Made with AI" labels hit real photos with minor edits and had to be renamed ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/07/01/meta-changes-its-label-from-made-with-ai-to-ai-info-to-indicate-use-of-ai-in-photos/)). Labelling some items also makes unlabelled items seem more credible, the implied-truth effect ([MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/2020/warning-labels-fake-news-trustworthy-0303)). Labels should therefore state facts: who captured the footage, when, what changed, and where the original is. They should not give verdicts.

PixelRoot's clearest market position follows from this table. It should not sell itself as a replacement for C2PA. It should be the **video-robust, attack-resistant soft binding** that (a) device makers ship to meet AB 853's 2028 capture-device option, (b) platforms read to meet AB 853's 2027 detect-and-surface duty, and (c) appears on the C2PA soft-binding algorithm list, so that EU-facing generators can use it as the "second technique" under the Code of Practice. The Code also makes providers prohibit stripping and offer detection. A scheme that anyone can verify, with no proprietary oracle, has a procurement advantage there over closed alternatives such as SynthID. One risk is that California's regime could face federal preemption litigation following the December 2025 executive order. No challenge specific to AB 853 had been found as of mid-October 2026 ([Jenner](https://www.jenner.com/en/news-insights/client-alerts/trump-administration-issues-executive-order-in-attempt-to-preempt-state-ai-laws)).

## Paper review: PixelRoot (IEEE TIFS-style)

I read the manuscript ([pixelroot.tex](/home/user/PixelRoot/paper/pixelroot.tex)), the code ([pixelroot.py](/home/user/PixelRoot/paper/experiments/pixelroot.py), [e1_robustness.py](/home/user/PixelRoot/paper/experiments/e1_robustness.py), [e2_security.py](/home/user/PixelRoot/paper/experiments/e2_security.py)) and the logged results ([ALL_RESULTS.txt](/home/user/PixelRoot/paper/experiments/results/ALL_RESULTS.txt)). I independently confirmed every issue the coordinator listed and add several more, marked *new* below.

### Summary of the submission

PixelRoot proposes that a CMOS sensor imprint a 128-bit payload (a 48-bit pseudonymous ID, a 32-bit timestamp and 48 bits of optional GPS) into every frame. The payload is first protected by a Reed–Solomon RS(48,16) code. It is then embedded as a keyed, antipodal spread-spectrum signal on the (0,2) DCT coefficient of 8×8 blocks, applied through per-column or per-region analog gain. At the same time, the device commits SHA-256(I‖m) to a Merkle-batched smart contract. Verification has two checks, V1 (is the hash on the ledger?) and V2 (does the in-pixel payload decode?), and four verdicts. The paper adds a feasibility survey of per-pixel gain (DCG/TCG, coded exposure, 3D stacking), an optional per-pixel tamper-localization layer, three propositions, and a privacy design. The experiments use 24 Kodak images with real libjpeg and WebP codecs. At A=4 (38.2 dB PSNR) the payload decodes on 23 of 24 images down to JPEG Q20 and 21 of 24 at Q10. Wrong-key and unmarked-image tests produce 0 of 1,224 false accepts. The localization layer reaches ROC-AUC 0.974. There is no silicon, no video, and no live ledger.

### The author's goals, and how well the submission meets them

The author's stated aim is a foundation for online provenance that is (1) all-encompassing for the problem, (2) robust, (3) extremely simple, (4) extremely reliable, and (5) works for video. The table grades the submission against each.

| Goal | Status in the submission | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| (1) All-encompassing | Covers authentic-capture photos. Absent: AI-generated content marking, edited or composited derivatives, live streams, audio, re-shared clips, and media from devices that never marked it | **Partial.** It covers one slice (new captures from future sensors) and does not connect to the synthetic-content side that the regulation actually mandates |
| (2) Robust | Strong against JPEG and WebP. Weak against geometry: Resize 0.5× gives 16/24, rotation ±1° gives 0/24, resized-crop 3% gives 0/24. Nothing tested on H.264, HEVC or AV1, platform round-trips, 9:16 reframing, regeneration or screen re-capture | **Not shown for the online setting.** Platform pipelines rescale and reframe, which is exactly where the scheme fails |
| (3) Extremely simple | Two bindings, three hardware tiers, three manufacturing routes, four verdicts, smart contract, zero-knowledge selective disclosure, per-pixel layer, symmetric-key oracle | **Not met.** The concept count is high, and several parts are unvalidated |
| (4) Extremely reliable | The copy attack succeeds 15/17. One fleet-wide symmetric key. A 32-bit prototype seed. Prop. 2 is contradicted by the code. Verdicts depend on a claimed m' that is not available after stripping | **Not met.** The scheme rarely gives a false accept on random input, but a forger can make it accept transplanted marks, which is the failure that matters |
| (5) Video foundation | One paragraph of design (§Video). Zero experiments. "Real-time" notarization conflicts with N=10⁴ batching | **Not met yet** |

### Strengths

The paper targets the right gap. Provenance that dies at the first upload is the main failure of today's ecosystem, as the Washington Post test showed. It treats C2PA as a complement rather than a rival. Moving from an earlier single-pixel spike design to a low-frequency spread-spectrum carrier with host suppression and soft-decision de-spreading is sound engineering, and the JPEG results are real and reproducible. The experiments use real codecs and a real RS library, report raw BER separately from post-ECC decode success, include an amplitude sweep with an honest failure cliff, and run a passive-PRNU baseline. The paper fails safe under laundering: heavy compression yields "unverified" rather than a false accept, and it explicitly says "unverified" must mean "unknown," which matches NSA and WITNESS guidance. The privacy section is careful: no raw PII on-chain, salted commitments, location off by default, pseudonymous IDs. The threats-to-validity paragraph and the explicit labelling of simulated components are commendable. The hardware-tier idea, in which a verdict reports the assurance level, matches C2PA's AL1/AL2 direction.

### Major weaknesses

**M1. The copy attack is a successful forgery, and the paper reports it as a defense.** E2 adds the marked residual of image A onto a different image B. The result decodes to A's payload in **15/17** trials ([ALL_RESULTS.txt](/home/user/PixelRoot/paper/experiments/results/ALL_RESULTS.txt)). Under the paper's own Definition 1, a forgery is any (I\*, m\*) accepted as Transcoded-consistent where I\* was not captured with m\*. The pair (B+residual, m_A) is exactly that. B now "verifies" as device A's capture at A's time and place. The paper calls this "copy exposed" (l.1291–1294). The reported "0/17 passes as a new identity" is a vacuous control. `claimedB` is a fresh random payload ([e2_security.py](/home/user/PixelRoot/paper/experiments/e2_security.py), l.89), so of course the transplanted mark never decodes to it. *New:* the attack is easier than E2 implies. E2 uses the true original to compute the residual. But the code makes every block a carrier (`R = n_blocks // len(coded)`, [e1_robustness.py](/home/user/PixelRoot/paper/experiments/e1_robustness.py), l.37), and the carrier basis (0,2) is public. A key-less attacker can therefore take one marked image, compute the host-suppressed (0,2) projection field with the decoder's own high-pass (`_project_field`), and add that field to any other image. No key, no averaging and no original are needed. The threat catalogue (Table III) lists neither the copy attack nor the averaging attack. Its claim that the PixelRoot mark resists the fate of PRNU-copy (l.1059–1068) is contradicted by the paper's own data.

**M2. Proposition 2 does not describe the implemented system, and the specified system conflicts with goal G3.** The paper defines the carrier seed as s=SHA-256(k‖m) (l.754, Alg. 1 l.847). The code instead calls `keyed_plan(key, …, salt=b"")`, which keys only on k ([pixelroot.py](/home/user/PixelRoot/paper/experiments/pixelroot.py), l.59–67). Neither version works. If the layout depends on m, V2 needs the claimed m' to regenerate the carriers. After metadata stripping, nothing supplies m': the on-chain object is an opaque salted Merkle root (l.1472–1476), so G3 ("metadata-independence") fails. If the layout depends only on k, as in the code, every image from an OEM shares one layout and one set of chips. Then averaging attacks (Yang et al.) and the single-image estimation in M1 apply, and Prop. 2's statement that "observing many images leaks nothing" is false. *New:* the combinatorial bound C(N_B, m)·2^m in Prop. 2 assumes the carrier *locations* are secret. The experiments use every block (m = N_B), so that factor is 1 and only the chip signs are secret, and M1 shows the chip signs need not be learned.

**M3. The single symmetric OEM key is a fleet-wide single point of failure, and V2 cannot be verified publicly.** One key k, shared across every device and needed by every verifier, means that one secure-element extraction, one insider, or one leaked verification-oracle key breaks every device of that OEM, in the past and the future, with no revocation story. The Nikon episode is what a key compromise costs even when keys are *per-device*. The paper admits V2 is "checked by a key-holding party" (l.1451–1466). That makes the in-pixel binding, which the paper presents as its central contribution, depend on an oracle that a sovereign or adversarial OEM controls. It also contradicts the abstract's "publicly verifiable" framing. The prototype's seed is reduced to **32 bits** (l.66), which is brute-forceable. The paper dismisses this as "an implementation detail" (l.758–762), but an attacker with one marked image can search all 2³² seeds offline and use the decode confidence to recognize the right one.

**M4. Screen re-capture of a deepfake yields a genuine credential (the analog hole).** The threat catalogue treats re-capture only as a back-dating problem (l.1046–1052). The real attack is different. Someone plays a deepfake on a high-quality display and films it with a genuine PixelRoot camera. The output carries a valid fresh mark and a valid on-chain commitment, and it verifies as Camera-original. This is the same weakness signature-based C2PA has, and Sony addresses it with 3D depth ([Sony](https://www.sony.eu/presscentre/enhancing-content-integrity-sony-launches-comprehensive-video-authenticity-verification-for-news-media)). The paper should state plainly that a valid credential proves "a sensor saw photons," and evaluate liveness or depth assertions, or else say it cannot address this.

**M5. There is no video evaluation, despite the title-level ambition.** §Video is one paragraph. E2 in the future-work list (H.264/HEVC vs QP and GOP) is not done. The relevant baselines show that codecs are the dominant failure mode (Video Seal falls to chance at CRF 45), so an image-only evaluation cannot support a claim to be a video foundation. *New:* social video reframes 16:9 to 9:16, removing about two-thirds of the width, and rescales 4K to 720p. The measured envelope already fails at a 3% resized-crop and a 0.5× resize. The scheme also has no synchronization template, and the block-grid search covers only ±8 px of translation.

**M6. Novelty is overstated against prior art the paper does not cite.** Table I claims PixelRoot alone has a hardware root, survives metadata stripping, and uses a public ledger. That ignores C2PA 2.1/2.2 Durable Content Credentials, which combine a hardware-signed manifest, a watermark and a fingerprint with a resolution API, and ProofMode or Starling, which add ledger anchoring. Digimarc's "Digital watermarking CMOS sensor" patents (US7688994, US8280098), "Digital watermarking image signals on-chip" (US8457346) and "Noise influenced watermarking" (US8565473) explicitly claim introducing a watermark during capture by "providing slight variations to physical characteristics" of pixels ([USPTO 7688994](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/7688994)). Blythe & Fridrich's "Secure Digital Camera" (DFRWS 2004) embedded camera, time and photographer identity at capture for chain of custody ([DFRWS](https://dfrws.org/presentation/secure-digital-camera/)). Mohanty et al. built CMOS watermarking chips. The bibliography also omits Cox et al. (1997) spread-spectrum watermarking, Hartung & Girod (1998) video spread-spectrum, Kutter et al. (2000) copy attack, Yang et al. (2024) averaging, Zhao et al. (2024) regeneration, TrustMark, Video Seal and SynthID. *Verified:* none of these appear in [references.bib](/home/user/PixelRoot/paper/references.bib). After narrowing, the defensible novelty is a codec-robust realization through existing column-gain hardware, plus a security analysis. That is a much narrower claim than the current one.

**M7. Several text claims contradict the paper's own measurements.** These are detailed in the Minor-issues table, but together they are major, because a TIFS reviewer who opens ALL_RESULTS.txt will find them immediately. The fidelity claim says ≥40 dB where the operating point is 38.2 dB. The geometric claims say "zero BER" where raw BER is never zero. The crop range is stated as 1–6% where only 1–3% was tested and the 2% crop failed. Table II hides weaker rows.

**M8. The hardware root and the ledger are unvalidated, yet they carry the title.** The sensor-gain path is a literature survey with schematics. It has no RAW/Bayer data, no ISP interaction study (fixed-pattern-noise correction, lens shading, HDR fusion and denoising may all erase or distort the mark), and no silicon. Notarization is simulated. "Real-time … within seconds" (abstract, G5) conflicts with Merkle batching at N=10⁴ and with the hybrid recommendation of OpenTimestamps-style anchoring, which takes hours. *New:* V1 hashes the *raw* frame I (l.823–826), but recipients receive JPEG, HEIC or MP4 files, so nobody downstream can recompute h unless the lossless raw is distributed. C2PA hashes the encoded asset for exactly this reason.

**M9. The per-pixel manufacturing section and the localization layer are off-thesis.** About 2.5 pages and three full-width figures argue for per-pixel gain, which the robust channel explicitly does not need. The tamper-localization layer works only on pristine or Q90 media (FPR 0.104 at Q90), so it does not help with online provenance. These sections dilute the paper and invite questions it cannot answer.

### Verdict and recommendation

**Recommendation: Major revision, leaning reject-and-resubmit.** The engineering of the JPEG-robust channel is competent, and the problem is important and timely. But the central security claim, that the in-pixel binding cannot be forged without the key, is contradicted by the paper's own copy-attack experiment and by its code's key-only layout. The title-level contributions (sensor hardware and blockchain notarization) are unvalidated, video is untested, and the novelty claim ignores Durable Content Credentials and twenty years of sensor-watermarking prior art. A resubmission should be a smaller, sharper paper. It should present a content-bound, collusion-resistant soft binding that points to a signed manifest, measured on real video platforms, with public verification.

## A simpler foundation: signatures for proof, pixels for lookup

The architecture that meets the author's five goals is *smaller* than the current paper. It works by giving each layer exactly one job and never asking a soft signal to act as proof.

**Layer 1: hardware-signed manifest as the hard binding, hashed per video segment.** At capture, the device's secure element signs a standard C2PA manifest with a per-device, attested, rotatable key. The certificate design should keep a user's captures unlinkable, as on Pixel 10. The key is never shared across a fleet. For video, each 1–2 s GOP-aligned segment (a CMAF fragment) is hashed. The hashes go into a Merkle tree whose root is signed, and live streams use C2PA 2.3 session keys. A trim on segment boundaries then still verifies against the signed root, which is how Sony's trim-preserving workflow should generalize. This one primitive delivers almost everything PixelRoot's V1 and its "hardware root" were meant to deliver. Unlike V1, it can be checked by anyone holding the distributed file, because it hashes the encoded asset.

**Layer 2: an in-pixel watermark that carries only a random pointer.** The payload should be a 64–96-bit random asset ID, drawn fresh for every asset (or every segment). It should contain no device ID, time or GPS. That removes the privacy problem entirely and leaves capacity for stronger error correction. The carrier should change per asset or per segment, for example through a per-asset permutation or shift of a public base pattern. Then averaging across a user's footage cancels rather than revealing a fleet-wide pattern. The detector can be **public**. Removal only yields "no information," so publishing the detector costs little, and public detection is what makes the system verifiable without an OEM oracle. Forgery is defeated by **content binding** rather than secrecy. The registry entry for an ID stores the signed manifest and a perceptual fingerprint of each segment. A verifier that reads ID *x* from footage checks that the footage's fingerprint matches the one registered under *x*. If someone transplants A's mark onto B (the E2 attack), B's fingerprint will not match and the result is "mark copied from another capture," not authentication. This is the Barr–Bradley–Hannigan defense to Kutter's attack, made concrete. PixelRoot's spread-spectrum DCT carrier, which is already good on JPEG, becomes this watermark once synchronization and video robustness are added. The column-gain actuator becomes an optional *Tier-A embedding location*, not the foundation.

**Layer 3: perceptual fingerprint as the backup lookup.** Per-segment video fingerprints (ISCC is already on the C2PA list) are registered at publication. When the watermark has been regenerated away, the fingerprint can still find candidate originals. Because fingerprints collide, a match is shown as "similar to registered capture X — view it," never as "authentic."

**Layer 4: a public, multi-operator transparency log instead of a mandatory blockchain.** Signed-manifest hashes, asset IDs and fingerprint hashes go into a Certificate-Transparency-style Merkle log. Several operators run it (for example newsrooms, a C2PA-affiliated body, a university and a civil-society group), and independent witnesses co-sign checkpoints. Inclusion proofs give trusted timestamps. A separate revocation log lets verifiers tell "signed before key compromise" from "signed after," which addresses the Nikon and timestamp-swap failures. The log's checkpoint can be anchored to a public chain every hour or so, OpenTimestamps-style, as an optional extra. Only hashes go into the log. Manifests are published by the owner when the owner chooses, which follows the WITNESS model of verification as "a choice, not an obligation."

**Layer 5: verification that reads the result honestly, with three states and a link to the original.** The three states are "Verified capture: [device class, assurance level, time] — view the original footage and edit history," "Verified AI-generated," and a **neutral** "No information." The link to the original serves the author's goal of "letting people access truthful footage." Even if a deepfake reuses frames from a real clip, a viewer can watch the registered original side by side. Revocation, timestamp checks and assurance levels are enforced in the verifier, not left to the specification.

**What to cut from the current paper for simplicity.** Cut the identity, time and GPS payload in pixels; replace it with a random ID. Cut the fleet-wide symmetric key and the OEM verification oracle; use per-device asymmetric signing and a public detector. Cut the mandatory smart contract and gas analysis; keep a log with optional anchoring. Cut the four-verdict lattice; use three states. Cut the per-pixel manufacturing argument and the localization layer; move them to an appendix or a follow-up paper. Cut the zero-knowledge selective-disclosure promise, which is unnecessary once nothing personal is in the pixels or the log. What remains could be explained in one paragraph: *sign every segment at capture; mark each frame with a pointer to that signature; look up by mark or fingerprint; check that the pointer matches the content; show the original.*

**What no provenance system can solve.** First, the *absence* of a credential proves nothing. For years most authentic footage, including much of the footage that matters most from bystanders and activists, will carry none. Any user interface that implies otherwise hands liars a dividend and penalizes the vulnerable. Second, the analog hole remains. A genuine camera filming a screen produces a genuine credential. Depth sensing, multi-camera consistency and liveness assertions raise the cost but do not close the hole. Third, adoption sets the ceiling. The system protects only footage from participating devices, uploaded through platforms that read and display provenance. The October 2025 Washington Post test shows how far platforms are from doing that, which is why AB 853's 2027 platform duty matters more than any algorithm. Fourth, provenance is not truth. A signed capture of a staged scene, or a real clip with a false caption, passes every check. Provenance tells people where footage came from and lets them find the original. Journalists, courts and viewers still decide what it means.

## Conclusion

The research shows that the bottleneck has moved. Capture-time signing exists in hardware, and the standards now cover live video and watermark-based recovery. What fails is survival through distribution and resistance to forgery of the signals meant to provide that survival. PixelRoot's real contribution, if it is reframed, sits exactly at that bottleneck. It would be an open, publicly detectable, collusion-resistant and content-bound watermark, robust to video codecs, that points to a signed segment manifest in a transparency log. It would be validated on the platforms where misinformation actually spreads. That version is simpler than the submitted paper and more reliable, because no single soft signal is ever trusted as proof. It is also better positioned for regulation: it fits California's 2028 capture-device option, the EU's two-technique floor and India's unique-identifier rule.

Two empirical results would show whether this approach works, and neither exists in the literature today. The first is measured survival of a capture-side mark through real TikTok, YouTube, Instagram and WhatsApp video pipelines, including 9:16 reframing. The second is a demonstration that content binding defeats both copy and averaging attacks at scale. If PixelRoot delivers those two results, it becomes a credible foundation component. If it keeps the current scope, it will be judged mainly against the Digimarc and Fridrich prior art and against its own copy-attack table.

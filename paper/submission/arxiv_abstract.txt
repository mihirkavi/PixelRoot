The realism of AI-generated "deepfake" imagery has outpaced society's ability to tell synthetic media from camera-captured reality. Detectors generalize poorly to unseen generators, and provenance schemes that bind trust to metadata fail once the metadata is stripped. We present PixelRoot, which moves the root of trust into the pixels and onto a public blockchain. At capture, a CMOS sensor embeds a 128-bit, Reed-Solomon-coded provenance payload through a keyed, low-frequency per-region gain modulation. Each sensor holds a signing key generated on-die and certified on the blockchain by its manufacturer; it signs a capture record that binds the payload to the frame's hashes and to a robust content fingerprint, and the record is notarized on the same blockchain, which also records time-scoped revocation. We contribute a formal architecture and threat model with five security propositions; a measured robustness study on the Kodak images with real codecs, recovering the payload on 22-23/24 images down to JPEG Q20 (21/24 at Q10) at 38 dB PSNR with 0/1224 false accepts; a device-identity evaluation showing that the in-pixel mark alone can be copied without any key (78% of trials) while the content-bound record rejects every cross-scene copy and large splice on held-out images (0/180) and lets 90% of processed genuine copies verify, linking local edits back to the original; a tested registry contract (47k gas per batch, O(1) per image); a CMOS-manufacturing feasibility analysis of per-pixel gain; and a privacy-preserving notarization design with a phased adoption path toward a cross-OEM interoperability standard.
